Copilot licences are the easy part.

Most customers can buy Copilot. The hard part is the next six months: finding use cases worth the effort, getting permissions into a state where Copilot is safe to switch on, and getting people to change how they work. That is the work I do.

Where Copilot rollouts stall

  • Licences without use cases. Everyone gets access, nobody knows what to try first, and usage drops after the first fortnight.
  • Data that isn't ready. Copilot surfaces whatever a user can technically reach. Oversharing that was invisible for years becomes visible on day one.
  • Adoption treated as a training day. People go back to old habits unless the examples come from their own work and someone keeps measuring.

A service ladder for Copilot

Four steps. Each is worth buying on its own, and each makes the next easier to sell and deliver. Durations are indicative for a mid-sized customer, and every step is scoped around an outcome agreed before work starts.

  1. Copilot readiness assessment

    Fixed fee, 1 to 2 weeks

    For
    Customers considering Copilot, or already licensed and seeing little use.
    They get
    A review of sharing and permissions across SharePoint and Teams, sensitivity label coverage, licence fit, a ranked list of use cases and a risk register.
    Success looks like
    The customer knows what to fix before rollout and which use cases to start with.

  2. Agent pilot

    Fixed scope, 4 to 6 weeks

    For
    Customers with one clear, valuable use case.
    They get
    One agent built in Copilot Studio and delivered in Teams, grounded in SharePoint or a backend data source. Success measures are agreed before the build, and the pilot ends with a go or no-go recommendation.
    Success looks like
    A working agent, measured against the targets set at the start.

  3. Adoption programme

    8 to 12 weeks

    For
    Customers with licences and low usage.
    They get
    Role-based sessions built on the customer's own documents and workflows, a champions network, a prompt and use-case library, and usage reporting reviewed monthly.
    Success looks like
    Sustained weekly usage in the teams that were targeted, not a one-off spike after training.

  4. Governed AI service

    Monthly

    For
    Customers with agents in production who need them kept safe, current and worth the cost.
    They get
    Agent lifecycle management, Power Platform data policies, consumption and cost monitoring, responsible AI review and a quarterly value report.
    Success looks like
    No surprises on cost or data exposure, and a clear view of what each agent returns.

When the out-of-the-box tools aren't enough

Some problems need Azure AI rather than Copilot: extracting structured data from large volumes of documents with Azure Content Understanding, or building retrieval over line-of-business data. I scope these as separate engagements, usually growing out of a pilot.

Adoption inside a seven-person Azure practice

At a Microsoft partner, I introduced Copilot and agent workflows through weekly content and hands-on coaching. Four colleagues have since adopted AI in their own work:

  • One has published a Microsoft agent.
  • One is applying AI to customer-facing AI design work.
  • Two are building a GitHub Copilot-driven Intune configuration reporting tool, with me coaching, to solve a problem the team has lived with for years. It turns a half-day-to-a-week review into a minute.

The weekly series continues to Christmas. The first post reached 41% of the channel's members (55 people), including two comments and seven reactions. That is a start rather than a result, and I'm reporting it as such.

Copilot adoption programme, run internally

Problem
At a Microsoft partner where every member of staff already held a Microsoft 365 Copilot licence, adoption had no dedicated owner and depended on people's spare time, which shrinks as the business grows. Most people used Copilot as an advanced search engine rather than a working partner, and many assumed they needed to be technical to get anything out of it.
What I did
Took on the adoption work myself and built a 10-part series of articles and short videos running up to Christmas, based on real use cases: how to use Copilot as a collaborator, how models and effort settings differ, and when to use one tool over another. A parallel strand covers GitHub Copilot for engineers, including document generation and using different models for different perspectives. With GitHub Copilot Enterprise accounts provided by practice leadership, I coach consultants on GitHub Copilot and the Git fundamentals it depends on: branches, commits and pull requests. I also built Microsoft agents for my own projects and shared them with the team. Between weeks three and five of the series, I'm recruiting champions across the company.
Outcome so far
The Azure practice had no AI capability before this started. Colleagues have since built their own agents, including one for estimating billable project effort, and another is putting a Microsoft 365 agent in front of a PowerShell script built with GitHub Copilot. Most began by solving a problem of their own and then sharing the result, which is the approach I encourage. The first post of the series reached 41% of the channel's members. I don't yet have usage data beyond my own team, so setting a company-wide baseline is the next thing to measure.

Document extraction with Azure Content Understanding

Problem
A public sector regulator had a backlog of around 7,500 documents and was struggling to keep up with new arrivals.
What I did
Built an ingestion pipeline on Azure: Event Hub for ingestion, Azure Content Understanding with a GPT model to extract the data, a Logic App to orchestrate, and an Azure Function to convert the output into SQL for Azure SQL Database. We started with small batches and increased volume as confidence in the pipeline grew.
Outcome
The backlog was cleared over six months and the process now runs largely unattended. Roughly 90% of documents flow from ingestion to SQL with no manual handling. The rest are edge cases, such as poor scans or the wrong document being submitted, and go to a person. All Azure resources together cost about £200 a month to run. Capacity is around 100 documents a day, up to about 1,000 a week, against an intake of 10 to 20 a day.
What I'd do next
Capacity is far above real intake, so a tuning review to right-size the pipeline was the planned next step.

Internal policy agent in Teams, on Foundry and Copilot Studio

Problem
Staff and new starters couldn't easily find answers in internal HR, finance and IT policies, so the same questions kept arriving as IT and HR tickets.
What I did
Built a retrieval (RAG) pipeline over the organisation's policies using Microsoft Foundry with Foundry IQ. I put the bot's day-to-day management in Copilot Studio, because the IT team running it didn't have access to the engineering side of Azure, and a no-code layer suited them. The agent is delivered in Teams, where people already work.
Outcome
IT and HR tickets fell from roughly 10 to 15 a month to 2 or 3. New starters use the agent to find out where to go for things, how holidays and sick leave work, and when to raise an IT service request rather than an incident, which made onboarding noticeably more efficient.
